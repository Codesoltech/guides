# Contact and Quote Forms That People Actually Finish

*By Sharafat Ali, CodeSol Technologies, Austin, TX.*

A visitor who opens your contact form has already decided to talk to you. That is the hardest part of the sale, and it is already done. Then the form itself talks them out of it. Too many fields, a question they cannot answer yet, an error message that wipes everything they typed, and they close the tab and call the next business on the list. The form was supposed to collect enquiries. Instead it filtered out the good ones.

Most forms on local business websites are built from the business side of the desk. Every department adds one field, the office wants the details upfront, and nobody ever fills the form in as a customer would, on a phone, standing in a kitchen, with a leaking pipe or a broken air conditioner in the background. A form that respects the visitor's time gets finished. A form that respects the office's paperwork gets abandoned.

The fix is not a prettier form. It is a shorter, clearer one that asks only what you need to reply, tells the visitor what happens next, and works properly on a phone. Below is how to get there, field by field.

## 1. Ask only what you need to reply

Every field on your form should pass one test: do you need this answer before you can call the person back? Name, phone number, what they need, and a short message pass that test. Almost everything else can wait for the call. You do not need their full address to reply to a quote request for a roof repair. You do not need their company size to answer a question about a cleaning schedule. You do not need their budget range before you have even said hello.

Each extra field costs you completions. A visitor who is ready to talk will answer four fields without thinking. The same visitor will stop at field nine and decide the phone call can wait until tomorrow, and tomorrow they will have forgotten your business name. If your form asks for more than seven fields, cut it in half and see what happens to your enquiries. In most cases the number goes up, not down, because the people who finish are the ones you can actually help.

There is one exception. If a field genuinely saves a wasted trip or a useless call, keep it. A pest control company that needs to know whether the problem is inside or outside the house is asking a useful question. A remodeling company that needs to know the size of the room is asking a useful question. The test stays the same: would you ask this question in the first minute of a phone call? If the answer is no, it does not belong on the form.

## 2. One form, one job

Contact forms and quote forms are two different tools, and mixing them into one form makes both worse. A visitor with a quick question does not want to answer quote questions about square footage and preferred dates. A visitor who wants a price does not want a generic "how can we help" box with no structure at all. When one form tries to serve both, it serves neither, and the visitor can tell within seconds.

Give each job its own form, and name it for the job. A contact form says "Send us a message" and asks for a name, a phone number or email, and a message. A quote form says "Get a quote" and asks for the service, a few details about the job, and how to reach the person. The buttons should match: "Send message" for one, "Request my quote" for the other. A visitor who sees a button that describes exactly what will happen next is far more likely to press it.

This also helps your team. A quote request that arrives with a clear service and a few job details can be priced or routed in minutes. A message that arrives through the right form already tells you whether it is a question, a complaint, or a new job. When every enquiry arrives through the same generic form, someone in the office has to read each one and guess what the visitor wanted. That guessing is slow, and slow replies lose jobs.

## 3. Make every field easy to fill on a phone

Most of your visitors are on a phone, and most of them are filling in your form with one thumb, often while doing something else. A form that is easy on a desktop and painful on a phone is a form that loses most of its enquiries, because the phone visitors are the majority.

Start with the basics. Labels should sit above each field, in plain words, and stay visible while the visitor types. Placeholder text inside the field is not a label: it disappears the moment someone starts typing, and then they cannot check what the field was asking for. A phone number field should bring up the number keyboard, an email field should bring up the email keyboard, and a date field should offer a date picker instead of asking someone to type a date by hand. These are small details, and they are the difference between a form that takes thirty seconds and a form that takes three minutes.

Check the size of everything. Fields should be tall enough to tap without hitting the field next to them, and the submit button should be big enough to press with a thumb, with enough space around it that a visitor does not tap the wrong thing. Text should be large enough to read without zooming. If you have to pinch and zoom to fill in your own form, your visitors are already gone.

## 4. Quote forms: give structure without an interrogation

A quote form has a harder job than a contact form. It has to collect enough detail for a useful price, without asking so much that the visitor gives up. The trick is to offer choices instead of blank boxes. A dropdown for the service, a dropdown for timing, and a short list of options for the most common variations will get you further than five open text boxes, because choosing is faster than writing, and faster means finished.

For a home service business, a good quote form usually needs four things: the service, a short description of the job, when they need it, and how to reach them. A photo upload can help for some jobs, such as a damaged roof or a broken appliance, but make it optional and say so clearly. A visitor who does not have a photo handy should never be blocked from asking for a quote. If the photo is required, say why, in one line, so the visitor understands the reason instead of feeling interrogated.

Do not ask for a budget on the form. Most visitors do not know what the job should cost, which is exactly why they are asking you. A budget field makes them guess, and a wrong guess either scares them off or anchors the conversation at the wrong number. Leave pricing to the conversation, where you can explain what drives the cost. The form only needs to start that conversation.

> Quick check: fill in your own quote form as if you were a customer who knows nothing about your trade. If you hesitate on any field, or you have to look something up before you can answer, that field is too hard. Rewrite it, make it optional, or remove it.

## 5. Tell them what happens next, before they press submit

The biggest reason people abandon a form is not the fields. It is uncertainty about what happens after they press submit. Will anyone reply? When? By phone or by email? Will they get a sales call every week for a year? A form that answers those questions near the submit button gets more submissions than a form that leaves them unanswered.

Write one or two plain sentences under the form, or right above the button, such as: "We reply within one business day, usually by phone. No spam, and we never share your details." If you can promise a faster reply, say so, and then keep the promise. A business that says "we reply within the hour" and replies the next day has broken a promise before the relationship even started. A business that says "within one business day" and replies in two hours has already impressed the customer.

The same honesty belongs on the thank-you page. After a visitor submits, show them a page that confirms the message arrived, repeats what happens next, and gives them a phone number in case they would rather call. A blank page, or a return to the homepage with no message at all, leaves the visitor wondering whether the form worked. A clear thank-you page turns a completed form into a customer who waits for your call instead of calling your competitor.

## 6. Errors that help instead of punish

Every form gets filled in wrong sometimes. A visitor mistypes an email address, leaves a required field empty, or enters a phone number in an unusual format. What happens next decides whether they fix it or leave. A good form points at the exact field with the problem, explains it in plain words, and keeps everything else the visitor already typed. A bad form shows a red message at the top, wipes the whole form, and asks the visitor to start again. Almost nobody starts again.

Check each field as the visitor leaves it, not only when they press submit, so a mistake is caught while the field is still fresh in their mind. Never clear a form after a failed submission. Never show an error that says only "invalid input" without saying what is wrong and how to fix it. And test the error messages yourself, on a phone, with a real mistake, because error messages written by developers often make perfect sense to developers and no sense at all to customers.

One more detail: do not make the email address the only way to reach someone. Many visitors prefer a phone call, and some will only give a phone number. If your form requires an email address and the visitor does not want to share one, you have lost an enquiry over a field you did not need. Ask for a phone number or an email, and let the visitor choose which one they give you.

## 7. Trust, placed where the visitor is deciding

A form asks a stranger for personal details. That is a trust decision, and it happens in the seconds before they press submit. A short privacy line near the button helps: "We use your details only to reply to your enquiry." If you have reviews, put a short line near the form, such as a star rating with a link to real reviews, rather than a paragraph of claims. A visitor who is deciding whether to trust you with their phone number wants proof, not promises.

Do not add a newsletter checkbox that is ticked by default, and do not add a marketing opt-in that is worded to confuse people into agreeing. A visitor who discovers they were signed up for emails they did not ask for will not become a customer, and may not come back. If you want people on your email list, ask them plainly, after the enquiry, when they already trust you.

Security also matters, and visitors can sense it. A form on a page without a secure connection, or a form that looks broken or outdated, will make a careful visitor stop. If your site has a security certificate problem, fix it before you worry about the form fields. No field layout can overcome a browser warning that tells the visitor the page is not safe.

## 8. The button is part of the form

The submit button deserves the same attention as the fields. A button that says "Submit" tells the visitor nothing about what happens next. A button that says "Send my message" or "Request my quote" tells them exactly what they are doing, and it matches the promise made above the form. Use the same words on the button as you use in the heading, so the whole form reads as one clear action.

Make the button visible without scrolling back up, and make it look like the most important thing on the form, because it is. On a long form, repeat the button or keep the form short enough that the button is never far away. After the button is pressed, show a clear loading state so the visitor knows the form is sending, and disable double submissions so an impatient double tap does not send the same enquiry twice.

## How this fits the rest of your website

A form does not stand alone. It is the last step of a page that has already done the selling, and the first step of a process that turns an enquiry into a job. A service page that explains the job clearly, shows proof, and answers the price question honestly sends visitors to the form already convinced. A change to the website, an update to a plugin, or a new spam filter can stop submissions without anyone noticing, and the business only finds out weeks later when the phone goes quiet. ## The form check you can run this week

1. **Open your contact form on your phone** and fill it in as a customer would, with one thumb, in under a minute.
2. **Count the fields.** If there are more than seven, mark every field that is not needed to reply, and remove it or make it optional.
3. **Check the labels and keyboards.** Is every label visible while you type? Does the phone field show a number keyboard?
4. **Break the form on purpose.** Submit it with a missing field and a wrong email address. Does it point at the problem and keep your answers?
5. **Read what happens next.** Does the form say when you will reply and how? Does the thank-you page confirm the message arrived?
6. **Ask one real customer** to fill in the form while you watch, and note where they pause. Every pause is a field to fix.

Most forms fail this check on the first try, usually on field count or on the thank-you page. That is useful news, not bad news. It means a shorter form, a clearer promise about what happens next, and an error message that helps instead of punishes can turn a form that filters people out into a form that brings enquiries in.
