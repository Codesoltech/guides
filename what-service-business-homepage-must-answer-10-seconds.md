# What a Service Business Homepage Must Answer in 10 Seconds

*By Sharafat Ali, CodeSol Technologies, Austin, TX.*

Most visitors do not read your homepage. They scan it. They land from a search, a map listing, or a recommendation, and within a few seconds they decide whether this business can help them, whether it looks trustworthy, and whether it is easy to contact. If those answers are not obvious at first glance, they go back to the search results and try the next business.

This is not about fancy design. It is about order. A homepage for a plumber, an HVAC company, a cleaning service, a salon, or a pest control firm has one job: turn a stranger with a problem into a caller or a booking. Everything on the page should serve that job, and anything that delays it should be moved, shortened, or removed.

Think of the first screen of your homepage, the part visible before anyone scrolls, as your shop window. Below are the seven questions it must answer, in the order visitors actually ask them.

## 1. What exactly do you do?

Your headline must say the service in plain words, not a slogan. "Quality solutions for all your needs" tells a visitor nothing. "Plumbing repair and installation in Austin" tells them everything they need to keep reading. If you offer several services, name the main ones in the headline area and link to the rest. Do not make a visitor guess whether you are the right kind of business.

Be specific about who the service is for. A residential cleaning company and a commercial cleaning company serve different buyers, even if the work looks similar. One line that names the customer, such as "for homeowners" or "for offices and retail spaces," saves the wrong visitors time and keeps the right ones reading.

## 2. Do you work in my area?

Local service is local by definition. A visitor who lives in Round Rock does not care that you also serve another city unless their own area is named. Put your service area near the top of the page, in plain text, not only inside a map graphic that search engines and screen readers cannot read easily. Name the cities or neighbourhoods you actually serve, and be honest about the edges. A visitor who finds out too late that you do not cover their area has wasted their time, and they will not come back.

If your home base is Austin, say so, but do not stop there. Customers search by their own suburb, not by your office address. A short line such as "Serving Austin, Round Rock, Cedar Park, and Georgetown" answers the question in one glance.

## 3. Can you handle my specific problem?

After the general service comes the specific job. A visitor with a leaking water heater, a blocked drain, or a broken air conditioner in July is not shopping for plumbing in general. They want to know you fix their exact problem. Your homepage should list the most common jobs you handle, with links to a service page for each one. That list does two jobs at once: it reassures the visitor, and it gives them somewhere useful to click next.

Keep the list short on the homepage. Five to eight common jobs is plenty. The full list belongs on your services page. The homepage list exists to help a visitor recognise their own problem in seconds.

## 4. Why should I trust you?

Trust is the reason a visitor picks one business over three similar ones. The strongest trust signals are simple and verifiable: real reviews, real photos of real work, years in business stated plainly, licences where they apply, and guarantees stated in words a customer can understand. A star rating with a link to the reviews behind it beats a paragraph claiming excellent service.

Use photos that show your own team, vehicles, and finished work, not generic stock images that could belong to any company. A visitor can tell the difference, even if they cannot explain why one page feels real and another feels hollow. One honest photo of a technician at work does more than a slideshow of smiling strangers.

> Quick check: if you removed your logo from your homepage, would a visitor still know it was your business? If the photos, reviews, and wording could belong to any competitor, your trust signals are too generic.

## 5. What do I do next?

Every homepage needs one clear next step, repeated in the same words wherever it appears. For most service businesses that step is a phone call or a quote request. Pick the one that brings you the best jobs, make it the biggest button on the page, and put it at the top, in the middle, and at the bottom. A visitor should never have to hunt for how to contact you.

Do not offer five competing actions at once. "Call, text, email, book online, chat, and download our app" is not helpful, it is a decision the visitor did not come to make. One main action, plus a quiet secondary option such as a contact form link, is enough. If you take bookings online, the booking button should say what happens after the click, for example "Book a visit" rather than just "Submit."

## 6. What will it cost, or how do I get a price?

Price is the question many service homepages avoid, and it is the question that sends visitors elsewhere. You do not need to publish a full price list to answer it. You do need to explain how pricing works: free estimates, flat rates for common jobs, hourly rates, or a visit fee that is credited against the work. Even a short line such as "Free estimates, no callout fee within Austin city limits" removes a major reason to leave.

If your prices genuinely vary by job, say that plainly and explain what the estimate covers. Visitors accept that a repair cost depends on the fault. What they do not accept is silence, because silence reads as "expensive" or "we will surprise you later."

## 7. How do I reach a real person?

A phone number in the header, visible on every page and tappable on a phone, is still the most important contact detail for a service business. Add your working hours next to it, and say what happens outside those hours: an emergency line, a form that is checked first thing in the morning, or a callback promise. A visitor with an urgent problem needs to know whether calling now will help, not discover at midnight that nobody answers until Monday.

Forms matter too, but keep them short. Name, phone number, service needed, and a message box will get more completions than a form asking for an address, a preferred date, a budget range, and three extra questions before the visitor has even spoken to you. You can collect the details on the call. The form only needs to start the conversation.

## The order matters as much as the answers

Put the answers in the order above: what you do, where you work, the jobs you handle, why you can be trusted, the next step, how pricing works, and how to reach you. Company history, awards, and mission statements can come lower down the page, after the visitor already knows you can help them. Nobody hires a plumber because of a mission statement. They hire because the plumber clearly fixes their problem, serves their area, and is easy to reach.

This order also shapes the rest of the site. A homepage that answers these seven questions well makes the job of every other page easier, because visitors arrive at service pages already convinced they are in the right place. ## What to leave off the homepage

- **Long company history.** A sentence is fine. A timeline belongs on an about page.
- **Every service you have ever offered.** List the common jobs and link to the rest.
- **Popups that appear before the visitor has read anything.** Ask for contact details after you have earned attention, not before.
- **Autoplaying video with sound.** It startles visitors and slows the page, especially on a phone.
- **Jargon.** If a customer would not say it on the phone, it does not belong in your headline.

Removing clutter is not losing content. It is moving the right content to the right page, where a visitor who wants it can find it without it blocking the visitor who just wants to call.

## Do not forget the phone in their pocket

Most local searches happen on a phone, often while the visitor is standing in front of the problem: a leaking pipe, a car that will not start, a salon chair they want to book. Test your homepage on a real phone, not only on a desktop screen. The phone number must be tappable, the main button must be reachable with a thumb, and the headline must be readable without zooming or sideways scrolling.

Speed counts here too. A homepage that takes several seconds to load on a mobile connection loses visitors before they read a single answer. Large uncompressed photos, extra animations, and widgets you never use all slow the page down. Keep the first screen light, and let the rest of the page load after it.

## The ten second test you can run this week

1. **Find five people** who do not work in your business, and show each of them your homepage for ten seconds, then hide it.
2. **Ask them four questions:** What does this business do? Where does it work? Would you trust it? What would you click to contact it?
3. **Write down every answer you did not want to hear.** Each wrong answer points to something on the first screen that needs moving, shortening, or rewriting.
4. **Fix the biggest miss first,** then run the test again. One clear fix beats a full redesign that nobody measures.

Most homepages fail this test on the first try, and that is useful news, not bad news. It means a few clear changes, headline, service area, trust signals, and one obvious next step, can improve the page more than months of small tweaks elsewhere.
